I haven't posted any lifehacks in a while.
Time to fix that.

Five lifehacks for junior PMs. They work for the not-so-junior ones too.

```diagram

mark: num
title: 5 lifehacks for junior PMs
- Questions | A document of questions and meetings to get answers
- English | Every day, in simple phrases, no "fancy" words
- Notes and calendar | Reminders, events and notes on everything
- Goal | Many goals, but one main goal
- Statistics | Numbers converted into money
```

## 1. Keep a document of questions

Start a document that lists the questions that come up while you work.

Put every work-related question in there:

- "Why are we doing this?"
- "What's the right way to do this?"
- "What's expected from this report?"
- "Who can I consult about this?"
- "Who should make the final decision on this?"
- "Can this be done faster, simpler, better?"
- "Can I delegate this part of the work?"
- "Who can help me with this issue or this task?"
- "Who can quickly give me the info I need for this document or report?"

Every now and then, schedule meetings with your manager, mentor or buddy to get answers to these questions.

This tip works for the not-so-junior ones too.

## 2. Learn English

How to practise:

- Start with the PMBOK Guide: write down every unfamiliar word and phrase in it and learn them.
- Switch all your social networks, apps, website interfaces and devices to English.
- Write documentation, task descriptions and reports in English (at least for yourself, if the team doesn't understand it or pushes back).
- Go to English speaking clubs, take the lead in conversations, practise spoken English as much as you can.

```visual
type: versus
title: Tips for learning English
left: Don't
right: Instead
lefticon: x
righticon: check
tone: bad-good
- Overcomplicate things | Simple phrases
- Forms you haven't practised | Present, Past and Future Simple
- Words you don't 100% understand | A few "simple" synonyms
- Spoken-language rules in emails | Formal email phrases
```

It's better to explain your idea with a few "simple" synonyms everyone understands than with one "fancy" word that confuses everyone.

The usual rules of spoken communication don't work for email. Memorise formal phrases: for opening an email, for the body in different situations (a request, a proposal, a report, an inquiry, reporting a problem and so on) and for closing it properly.

Use ChatGPT, Google Translate, DeepL, Grammarly and online lessons on YouTube to improve your grammar. Hire tutors or ask a friend to help you improve your pronunciation and get rid of your accent.

English is one of the [10 key skills of an IT project manager](/blog/it-project-manager-skills-from-job-postings/).

This tip works for the not-so-junior ones too. And even for non-PMs.

## 3. Write everything down: reminders, calendar, notes

Write everything down in notes or put it in your calendar.

Get into the habit of setting reminders, and use a calendar with events.

```diagram
type: groups
title: Reminders and calendar
# Set a reminder
- You agreed to call someone
- You set a task with a deadline
- A few minutes before an important meeting, to prepare
- After the working day, to sum it up
# Keep a calendar
- All meetings with stakeholders (and your team)
- Subscribe to key calendars of colleagues and managers
- Block time for lunch, focus and personal matters
- Use "find a time" for meetings with very busy people
```

Write everything down, always:

- Take notes after every meeting, for yourself and for the team.
- Keep the requests, questions and tasks people give you in a separate list. Delete an item once it's closed.
- Take screenshots and screen recordings, send yourself voice and text messages.
- Use tools that sync across devices and are easy to open on both your computer and your phone (Google Docs, Apple Notes). Or store information in messengers by sending messages to yourself.

Memory can let you down. Notes and reminders often save the day.

Relying on someone else's memory is even riskier than relying on your own.

AI agents can help you, but you have to check their work so they don't hallucinate.

## 4. Always have a goal

It's easy to drown in routine, get lost among different lines of work and forget your own needs. Having a goal protects you from that.

Goals can vary:

- Get to grips with project documentation
- Get to grips with processes
- Get to grips with risk management
- Get to grips with planning
- Improve soft skills and communication
- Work for career growth
- Work to learn from the company
- Work to earn money

You can have many goals at once, but there should be one main goal.

```diagram
type: cycle
center: One main goal
- Set a goal
- Check every action against it
- Reach it
- See what has changed
- Set a new one
```

### Stick to your goal and reach it

Every action should first be checked against these questions:

- Does this help me reach the goal?
- Will not doing this get in the way of the goal?
- Does this action or decision take me further from my goal?

When you check against your goal, it's much easier to resolve a conflict even if it doesn't go your way, to take on a "hard and boring" task, and simply not to do anything stupid.

### Reached the goal? Set a new one

- Based on the fact that your skills have already changed
- Based on the fact that your expertise has grown
- Based on the fact that your needs and requirements are different now
- Taking into account what you've learned and discovered

At the start, there's a lot we don't know, so we can't plan or set goals.

With every step of growth, we see better what lies ahead.

Pick the best and most interesting of what you see in front of you, make it your new goal and go for it!

## 5. Collect statistics and convert them into money

Collect statistics. Lots of statistics!

- How much time meetings take (yours and your colleagues')
- How many times people come to you for clarifications on a task
- How often completed tasks come back for bug fixes
- How far the initial estimate is from the actual one
- Who successfully completed how many tasks in a month, quarter, year
- How much time is spent waiting for merges and deploys
- How many hours a month you spend on reporting
- What share of your reports repeats previous ones without significant changes
- How much time it takes on average to get clarifications from the client or the main stakeholder
- How time is split between design, preparing requirements, writing task descriptions, development, testing and bug fixing
- Any other statistic you can measure or calculate about everything you and your team do

```visual
type: flow
title: From numbers to an argument
end: effect
- Collect | everything you can measure or calculate | chart
- Convert into money | dry numbers as a money equivalent | money
- Use them | negotiations, processes, career | up
```

These statistics are your support, your protection and your strongest argument in any negotiation.

These statistics let you avoid repeated mistakes, optimise processes, and improve efficiency and productivity.

These statistics open up simple and fast paths to career growth and nice cash bonuses.

Learn to work with data and statistics, because later it will be simply impossible to grow and develop your career without it.
